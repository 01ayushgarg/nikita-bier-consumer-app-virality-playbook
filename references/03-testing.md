# 03 · Testing like a scientist

## The process beats the idea

> "The most important thing that I often instruct teams to do is to develop a reproducible
> testing process, and that will actually influence the probability of your success more than
> anything." (19:08)

> "It's so unpredictable whether a consumer product idea will work. If you actually focus more
> on your process for taking many shots at bat, that's what actually reduces the risk more than
> anything." (19:21)

Their speed improved with practice: the first mobile app took about a year, the last about two
weeks (18:54). 15 apps, 14 "basically duds" (15:01), before tbh.

## Remove confounding variables

> "You never want to walk away from an experiment or test and say, 'Well, maybe the execution
> was bad.'" (31:58)

Test the best possible version of the thing, even with manual work that won't scale, so a
"no" really means no. For a social app that means making sure every tester has friends on it:
"we would try to get an entire school to adopt, just to know if everyone had 10 friends, would
they actually derive value from this app?" (32:34)

## The seeding method (for TESTS, not growth)

- People need to see a marketing message "three times or so" before downloading, so saturate
  one area with every kind of marketing (29:44 to 30:06).
- School-targeted ads, plus a dedicated Instagram account for that school that followed
  students, because "high schoolers identify their school in their bio" (30:13 to 30:33).
- **His caveat, keep it:** "This is not the way we grew the app. This is how we tested apps."
  It was for the first 100 users; "the app should grow by itself after that." (30:43 to 31:16)
- tbh's launch school was chosen as the one with the earliest start date in the US (21:18).
  About 40% of that school downloaded it in the first 24 hours (21:26).

## Live chat support as user research

> "Put live chat customer support in your app 24 hours a day." (32:46)

Two reasons: it removes another confounding variable (users feel helped), and "it's the best
vehicle for getting feedback and doing user research because users will literally tell you the
problem they're having." (33:18) Their support lead pasted interesting feedback into Slack for
the team to act on.

**Checks to run:**
- Is there a repeatable way to launch a test (where, to whom, how fast)?
- Did the last failed test actually test the idea, or was it starved of density, support or polish?
- Is the yes/no signal agreed BEFORE the test?
- Is a human reading every support message during a test?
