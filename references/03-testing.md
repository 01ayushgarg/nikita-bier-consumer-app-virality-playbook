# 03 · Testing like a scientist

## The process beats the idea

> "The most important thing that I often instruct teams to do is to develop a reproducible
> testing process, and that will actually influence the probability of your success more than
> anything." [LP 19:08]

> "It's so unpredictable whether a consumer product idea will work. If you actually focus more
> on your process for taking many shots at bat, that's what actually reduces the risk more than
> anything." [LP 19:21]

Their speed improved with practice: the first mobile app took about a year, the last about two
weeks [LP 18:54]. 15 apps, 14 "basically duds" [LP 15:01], before tbh.

## Remove confounding variables

> "You never want to walk away from an experiment or test and say, 'Well, maybe the execution
> was bad.'" [LP 31:58]

Test the best possible version of the thing, even with manual work that won't scale, so a
"no" really means no. For a social app that means making sure every tester has friends on it:
"we would try to get an entire school to adopt, just to know if everyone had 10 friends, would
they actually derive value from this app?" [LP 32:34]

## The seeding method (for TESTS, not growth)

- People need to see a marketing message "three times or so" before downloading, so saturate
  one area with every kind of marketing [LP 29:44 to 30:06].
- School-targeted ads, plus a dedicated Instagram account for that school that followed
  students, because "high schoolers identify their school in their bio" [LP 30:13 to 30:33].
- **His caveat, keep it:** "This is not the way we grew the app. This is how we tested apps."
  It was for the first 100 users; "the app should grow by itself after that." [LP 30:43 to 31:16]
- tbh's launch school was chosen as the one with the earliest start date in the US [LP 21:18].
  About 40% of that school downloaded it in the first 24 hours [LP 21:26].

## Assume you're wrong, and build reusable parts

[X 2021-07-09](https://x.com/nikitabier/status/1413392823630680071):

> "If you're building a social app, you should assume by default that your idea is wrong and map out
> the possible pivots. Then use this pivot map to create reusable building blocks (such as the
> friendfinder, invites, onboarding system). It will save you months, if not years."

[X 2023-05-25](https://x.com/nikitabier/status/1661733445163417601):

> "The components that have the most engineering scope tend to be wholly reusable—such as the
> friendfinder & invite system. The fastest way to arrive at an app that resonates is to run rapid
> experiments by only changing the interaction model"

## Against "minimum viable products"

[X 2024-11-16](https://x.com/nikitabier/status/1857896428317630893):

> "More and more, I have lost conviction that "minimum viable products" make sense for product
> development. It makes no sense to release a product with the core flow and then dismiss its
> viability after the aggregate data says people aren't using it. Instead, founders should have a
> fundamental belief about what people want—and they should keep iterating until that value is
> correctly surfaced. In practice, this means you should keep a steady flow of new users as you add
> components to the product, seeing if it solves a core part of the activation and signup loop. The
> only part that could possibly be "half-baked" is those individual components. But even those need
> sufficient quality so there are no confounding factors that distort the signal."

## Time-box the attempt

[X 2023-10-01](https://x.com/nikitabier/status/1708513023990645011):

> "If I was putting together a team to make a new viral app, the expectations would be simple: You
> will work for 10 hours a day for 7 days a week and put all other life plans on hold for 4 months. In
> exchange, you will own double digit equity, draw no salary, but 3 meals a day will be provided. If it
> doesn't start working by the end of 4 months, we disband."

## Live chat support as user research

> "Put live chat customer support in your app 24 hours a day." [LP 32:46]

Two reasons: it removes another confounding variable (users feel helped), and "it's the best
vehicle for getting feedback and doing user research because users will literally tell you the
problem they're having." [LP 33:18] Their support lead pasted interesting feedback into Slack for
the team to act on.

**Checks to run:**
- Is there a repeatable way to launch a test (where, to whom, how fast)?
- Did the last failed test actually test the idea, or was it starved of density, support or polish?
- Is the yes/no signal agreed BEFORE the test?
- Is a human reading every support message during a test?
