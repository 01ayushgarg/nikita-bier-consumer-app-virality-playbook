# 19 · What he learned at Facebook

He spent about four years at Facebook after it bought tbh, building and testing zero-to-one apps
and advising Instagram and Messenger [OOO 57:50 to 58:00]. Lenny's Podcast opens with this period,
and it explains several of his habits: designing in the pixels, distrust of process, and blunt
honesty about why people use apps.

## A science of growth already existed

> "it's like the harvard of social networks." [LP 39:02]

He spent his time reading internal studies of the form "if we change that, this is the impact to
retention and dau" [LP 39:07].

He was impressed that "a lot of the stuff that we did was learned from first principles" turned
out to be written-down systems and processes there [LP 39:15].

## But the PM was far from the product

> "the thing i didn't realize as a product manager in a large tech company is there is very little
> product management that you do." [LP 39:29]

He had expected to be in the pixels and the flows and found design ran in its own organisation [LP
39:47 to 39:59]. His self-description: "at the core, i'm a designer. i don't consider myself a
product manager. i'm great at growing things, looking at mixed panel and then designing the things
that make it grow." [LP 40:11] He says "a lot of my skills atrophied over those four years." [LP 40:38]

His summary in the episode's opening: "products live and die in the pixels. you should be designing
the hierarchy, the pixels, the flows, everything." [LP 1:07]

## New apps are harder inside a big company

> "it is much, much more difficult to build apps at a large company." [LP 41:12]

The reason he gives is honesty: the real motivation behind an app ("we're building an app for teens
to flirt") is not something you can put in a VP review, "and when the team isn't honest about it,
then it's really hard to iterate toward the right thing" [LP 41:17 to 41:47]. In 2022 he said big
tech's weak spot is "zero to one creating new products" [WIH 11:59], and that large companies take
12 to 24 months to respond to a new competitor [LP 45:53].

What a big company does give you: no money worries, distribution, and a legal and regulatory
apparatus that, as a small founder, he had “zero exposure to” [LP 41:54 to 42:06]. On distribution he
is dismissive: getting an app into a dense friend graph “is overall trivial” for a founder after enough
tries [LP 44:15].

## Speed

Comparing X with Facebook: "we build it in a week and it's it's out, you know, at facebook things
would take months to get approvals." [OOO 41:19]

## Careers

He noticed peers aspiring to be VPs with large teams, then met VPs who "were actually jealous of me"
because he got to build something high-impact with a small team [LP 1:20:32 to 1:20:45].

## How to apply it (our reading)

**If you are a founder:**
- Borrow the big-company habit (write down what each change did to retention), not the big-company
  structure. A one-page experiment log per test is enough.
- Keep the person who reads the funnel and the person who designs the screen the same person, or
  sitting together.
- Write the honest one-line motivation for your app somewhere the team sees it, even if it would
  never go in a deck.

**If you are inside a large company building something new:**
- Get a small, separate team with the right to state the real user motivation plainly.
- Agree the test, thresholds and decision date up front so approvals don't stretch a 48-hour read
  into a quarter.
- Use the parent app's distribution only for tests; judge the product on whether it spreads without it.

**Failure modes.**
- Treating process documents as progress.
- A product brief that hides why people would really use it.
- Assuming distribution from a parent app proves demand.

**Limits.** This is his experience at one company, 2017 to 2021, as he tells it. Other people at
the same company may describe it differently.
